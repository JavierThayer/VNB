cauchy-schwarz-finite

forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr)], finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * b(i)), s) * finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * b(i)), s) <= finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * a(i)), s) * finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, b(i) * b(i)), s)))

In words: For any finite index set S and real vectors a, b on S: the square of the dot product SUM a(i)b(i) is at most the product of the two squared norms, (SUM a(i)^2)(SUM b(i)^2). Cauchy-Schwarz for finite sums, square form.

Warrant (well-known): Cauchy-Schwarz for finite sums: (SUM a_i b_i)^2 <= (SUM a_i^2)(SUM b_i^2). The difference is the Lagrange identity SUM_{i<j} (a_i b_j - a_j b_i)^2 >= 0 (a finite sum of squares). Square form -- the root form is cauchy-schwarz-sqrt below (now that SQRT exists, real-powers.scm).

cauchy-schwarz-sqrt

forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr)], abs(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * b(i)), s)) <= sqrt(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * a(i)), s)) * sqrt(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, b(i) * b(i)), s))))

Warrant (well-known): Cauchy-Schwarz, root form: |SUM a_i b_i| <= sqrt(SUM a_i^2) * sqrt(SUM b_i^2). Take square roots in cauchy-schwarz-finite (both sides nonnegative); the left side is bounded using |SUM| <= sqrt((SUM)^2).

holder-finite

forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr), p], p in qq and 1 < p implies forall([q], q in qq and 1 < q implies (/)(1, p) + (/)(1, q) = 1 implies finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, abs(a(i) * b(i))), s) <= rpow(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, rpow(abs(a(i)), p)), s), (/)(1, p)) * rpow(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, rpow(abs(b(i)), q)), s), (/)(1, q)))))

In words: For any finite index set S, real vectors a, b on S, and conjugate exponents p,q>1 (rational, 1/p+1/q=1): the sum of |a(i)*b(i)| over S is at most the product of the p-norm of a and the q-norm of b -- (SUM |a_i|^p)^(1/p) times (SUM |b_i|^q)^(1/q). Holder's inequality for finite sums.

Warrant (well-known): Hoelder's inequality: SUM |a_i b_i| <= (SUM |a_i|^p)^(1/p) (SUM |b_i|^q)^(1/q) for conjugate exponents 1/p+1/q=1 (p,q>1). Termwise Young (young-inequality) on the normalised vectors, summed. Cauchy-Schwarz is the p=q=2 case. Zero entries are handled by the 0^p=0 convention (rpow-zero-base).

minkowski-l2

forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr)], sqrt(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, (a(i) + b(i)) * (a(i) + b(i))), s)) <= sqrt(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * a(i)), s)) + sqrt(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, b(i) * b(i)), s))))

In words: For any finite index set S and real vectors a, b on S: the Euclidean norm of the coordinatewise sum, sqrt(SUM (a_i+b_i)^2), is at most the sum of the Euclidean norms sqrt(SUM a_i^2) + sqrt(SUM b_i^2). The l^2 triangle inequality.

Warrant (well-known): Minkowski (l^2 triangle inequality): the Euclidean norm sqrt(SUM (a_i+b_i)^2) <= sqrt(SUM a_i^2) + sqrt(SUM b_i^2). Square and apply cauchy-schwarz-sqrt to the cross term. This is the triangle inequality for the Euclidean metric on R^n.

Combinatorial constructions (4)

choose-times-factorial

forall([n in nn, m], m in nn and m <= n implies choose(n, m) * factorial(m) = falling(n, m))

Warrant (well-known): Both sides count the injections of an m-element set into an n-element set. (i) Each injection ORD-SEGMENT(m) -> ORD-SEGMENT(n) factors uniquely as a choice of m-element image A subset ORD-SEGMENT(n) (CHOOSE(n,m) of them, with card-image-injection making |A| = m) followed by a bijection ORD-SEGMENT(m) onto A (m! = CARD(PERMUTATIONS m) of them); so the count is CHOOSE(n,m) * m!. (ii) injection-count-falling gives the same count as FALLING(n,m) = n(n-1)...(n-m+1). Equate the two counts.

injection-count-falling

forall([n in nn, m], m in nn and m <= n implies card(injection(ord-segment(m), ord-segment(n))) = falling(n, m))

Warrant (well-known): Induction on m via injection-extension-recurrence. Base: m = 0,

INJECTION(EMPTY-SET, C)= 1 = FALLING(n,0) (injection-from-empty,

ORD-SEGMENT 0 = EMPTY-SET). Step: a domain of size m into a codomain of size n leaves n - m free targets, so extending the domain by one point multiplies the count by (n - m): exactly FALLING(n,succ m) = (n-m)*FALLING(n,m).

injection-extension-recurrence

forall([a, c, b, m], not(b in a) and a in set and c in set and card(a) in nn and m in nn and card(c) = card(a) + m implies card(injection(union(a, pair(b, b)), c)) = m * card(injection(a, c)))

Warrant (informal): Counting injections by extending the domain one point. An injection from a union {b} into c restricts to an injection from a into c, and then sends b to any c-point not already in the image; card(a) points are used, leaving card(c) - card(a) = m choices, independent of the restriction. Hence m * card(injection(a, c)). The subtraction-free hypothesis card(c) = card(a) + m supplies m directly.

permutation-recurrence

forall([n in nn], card(permutations(succ(n))) = succ(n) * card(permutations(n)))

Warrant (informal): The factorial recurrence. A permutation of a succ(n)-element set is fixed by the image of the new point (succ(n) choices) together with a permutation of the remaining n (card(permutations(n)) each), and the two choices are independent: card(permutations(succ n)) = succ(n) * card(permutations(n)). Base case permutations-zero.

Real analysis: limits, series, completeness (7)

absolute-summable-implies-summable

forall([grp], is-normed-ag(grp) and is-complete(nag-metric-space(grp)) implies forall([f], f in fun(dom(f), carr(grp)) and is-absolutely-summable(grp, f) implies is-summable(grp, f)))

Warrant (informal): Absolute summability makes the norm-tails vanish: ESUM(i |-> ||f(i)||) is a real, so by esum-finite-iff-bounded its finite partial sums are bounded and approach their sup -- for every eps there is a finite fin <= DOM f with Sum_{DOM f minus fin} ||f|| < eps. Then for any finite ext with fin <= ext <= DOM f, ||Sum_ext f - Sum_fin f|| <= Sum_{ext minus fin} ||f|| < eps by the triangle inequality (subadditivity of the group norm). Hence the net of finite partial sums is Cauchy in NAG-METRIC-SPACE(grp); completeness (IS-COMPLETE) supplies a limit r in the carrier, and that r witnesses SUMS-TO(grp, f, r), so IS-SUMMABLE(grp, f). The sum is unconditional by construction (SUMS-TO is a net over finite subsets, not a series). A candidate to discharge into a formal `proof' later.

closed-graph-theorem

forall([m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt], is-frechet-structure(m1, fam1) implies is-frechet-structure(m2, fam2) implies has-closed-graph(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt) implies is-cont-lin(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt))

In words: Closed graph theorem: a CLOSED linear map tt between Frechet spaces -- closed meaning precisely that its graph is closed, i.e. seq -> p1 and tt(seq) -> p2 force p2 = tt(p1) -- is continuous. A corollary of the open mapping theorem: the graph G is a closed subspace of the product, projG->E is a continuous linear bijection, so its inverse is continuous and tt = projG->F o (projG->E)^-1. Sources: the user's TVS notes Thm 4.9; Yosida II.6. Uses Baire.

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Topological Vector Spaces (notes), Theorem 4.9

dual-is-normed-vector-space

forall([m], is-normed-vector-space(m) implies is-normed-vector-space(dual(m)))

In words: For every normed vector space m, the tuple DUAL(m) -- the bounded linear functionals on m, with pointwise addition, negation and real scaling, and the operator norm DUAL-NORM -- is itself a normed vector space.

Warrant (reference): The dual of a normed vector space is a normed vector space. The bounded linear functionals are closed under pointwise sum, negation and real scaling (each operation preserves additivity, homogeneity and boundedness, the bound constants adding resp. scaling), so DUAL-VEC(m) with those operations is a real vector space with the zero functional as its zero. The operator norm is a norm on it: DUAL-NORM(m,f) >= 0 by definition; DUAL-NORM(m,f) = 0 forces

f(x)<= 0 for every x, hence f = 0; DUAL-NORM(m, r.f) =rDUAL-NORM(m,f)

since c bounds |f| iff |r|c bounds |r f|; and DUAL-NORM(m, f+g) <= DUAL-NORM(m,f) + DUAL-NORM(m,g) because the sum of the two bounds is a bound for the sum and DUAL-NORM is the LEAST bound. Standard: Rudin, Functional Analysis, 4.1; Conway, A Course in Functional Analysis, III.1.

hahn-banach-seminorm

forall([m, p, s, f, fld], is-module(m) implies is-normed-field(fld) implies scal(m) = normed-field-as-commutative-ring(fld) implies is-seminorm(m, p) implies is-submodule(m, s) implies is-k-linear-on(m, s, f) implies forall([x in s], (fnrm(fld))(f(x)) <= p(x)) implies forsome([ff], is-k-linear(m, ff) and extends-on(s, ff, f) and forall([x in vec(m)], (fnrm(fld))(ff(x)) <= p(x))))

In words: Hahn-Banach, seminorm-dominated form, over K = RR or CC. Let m be a K-vector space -- a module whose scalars are the normed field fld, SCAL(m) holding fld's six-slot ring view -- p a seminorm on m, s a subspace, and f a K-linear functional on s with |f(x)| <= p(x) for x in s, |.| = FNRM(fld). Then f extends to a K-linear functional ff on all of m with |ff(x)| <= p(x) everywhere. The proof is the classic transfinite/Zorn extension (one dimension at a time, dominated bound preserved); over CC it is the Bohnenblust-Sobczyk reduction to the real part. Sources: Yosida IV.1; the user's notes (exercise).

Warrant (reference): Yosida, Functional Analysis, Hahn-Banach Extension Theorem, Ch. IV.1

open-mapping-bounded-inverse

forall([m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt, gg], is-frechet-structure(m1, fam1) implies is-frechet-structure(m2, fam2) implies is-cont-lin(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt) implies is-linear-map(m2, m1, gg) implies forall([x in vec(m1)], gg(tt(x)) = x) implies forall([y in vec(m2)], tt(gg(y)) = y) implies is-cont-lin(m2, fam2, m1, fam1, gg))

In words: Open mapping theorem, bounded-inverse form: a continuous linear bijection tt between Frechet spaces (with linear two-sided inverse gg) has continuous inverse gg. A corollary of `open-mapping-theorem': openness of tt is continuity of gg. Sources: Yosida II.5; the user's TVS notes, Thm 4.7. Uses Baire.

Warrant (reference): Yosida, Functional Analysis, Corollary of the open mapping theorem, Ch. II.6

open-mapping-theorem

forall([m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt], is-frechet-structure(m1, fam1) implies is-frechet-structure(m2, fam2) implies is-cont-lin(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt) implies forall([y in vec(m2)], forsome([x in vec(m1)], y = tt(x))) implies is-open-lin-map(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt))

In words: Open mapping theorem (Banach): a continuous linear surjection tt between Frechet spaces is open. Stated where openness of a LINEAR map actually lives -- at 0: the image of the basic neighbourhood FR-BALL(m1, fam1, n, eps) contains a basic neighbourhood FR-BALL(m2, fam2, n', eps'). No open-set quantification is needed and none occurs in the proof. In Schaefer's vocabulary (III.1, p. 74) this says tt is a TOPOLOGICAL HOMOMORPHISM onto m2. Sources: the user's TVS notes Thm 4.7; Schaefer III.2 (Banach's homomorphism theorem, p. 76); Yosida II.5 (p. 75, for F-spaces, i.e. without local convexity). Those three prove more than is stated here: the codomain need only be a Hausdorff TVS and tt's RANGE need only be non-meager, whence tt is surjective and the codomain complete metrizable. Here surjectivity is assumed and the codomain is assumed Frechet -- meagerness relative to a seminorm family is not in the base. Uses Baire.

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Topological Vector Spaces (notes), Theorem 4.7

Set & quotient constructions (4)

caratheodory-measurable-sigma-algebra

forall([omega, mu], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies is-sigma-algebra(omega, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu)))

In words: Caratheodory's theorem, first half: the sets that split every test set additively for an outer measure mu form a sigma-algebra on omega. Thayer Prop. 4.4 (p. 30) states both halves in one sentence ('the mu* measurable sets form a (non-empty) sigma-algebra A and mu*|A is a countably additive measure'); VNB splits it into this and caratheodory-measure, because the two conclusions are about different objects and a proof will want them separately. Rudin does not carry the Caratheodory construction in Chapter 1, so no Rudin number is given.

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 4.4

caratheodory-measure

forall([omega, mu], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies is-measure(omega, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu), res(mu, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu))))

In words: Caratheodory's theorem, second half: the restriction of the outer measure mu to the sigma-algebra of its measurable sets is a countably additive measure. Thayer Prop. 4.4 (p. 30), written there as mu*|A; VNB writes the restriction with the kernel's RES head. See caratheodory-measurable-sigma-algebra for why the source's one statement is two here.

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 4.4

caratheodory-null-measurable

forall([omega, mu, es], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies es subset omega implies mu(es) = 0 implies is-caratheodory-measurable(omega, mu, es))

In words: Every set of outer measure zero is Caratheodory measurable. Thayer Remark 4.3 (p. 30), stated there as 'every set E such that mu*(E) = 0 is measurable'; VNB adds the explicit hypothesis that es is a subset of omega, which the source carries implicitly by working inside P(X).

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Remark 4.3

caratheodory-superadditive-suffices

forall([omega, mu, es], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies es subset omega implies forall([a_ in power(omega)], eplus(mu(intersection(a_, es)), mu(difference(a_, es))) <= mu(a_)) implies is-caratheodory-measurable(omega, mu, es))

In words: Only one of the two inequalities has to be checked: if the two pieces of every test set together weigh AT MOST the test set, then es is Caratheodory measurable, the reverse inequality being automatic from subadditivity. Thayer states this as the displayed inequality (75) in the paragraph after Def. 4.2 (p. 30) -- an unnumbered remark, so the citation is to the equation, not to a numbered result. This is the working form of the measurability test and the reason it is stated at all.

Warrant (reference): Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Eq. (75), following Def. 4.2

bounded-metrizable-iff-gauge-countable

forall([s], is-top-space(s) implies forsome([md], is-bounded-metric-space(md) and metric-top(md) == s) iff is-hausdorff(s) and is-gauge-countable(s))

Warrant (well-known): Metrizable by a bounded metric iff Hausdorff and gauge-countable. Immediate corollary of metrizable-iff-bounded-metrizable (T1) and metrizable-iff-gauge-countable (T2): compose the two equivalences through metrizability.

gauge-countable-not-metrizable-witness

forsome([s], is-gauge-countable(s) and not(is-metrizable-top-space(s)))

Warrant (well-known): There is a gauge-countable space that is not metrizable, so IS-GAUGE-COUNTABLE without IS-HAUSDORFF does not imply IS-METRIZABLE-TOP-SPACE. Concrete witness: the single pseudometric d == 0 on any carrier with at least two points is a countable (singleton) pseudometric family whose gauge topology is the indiscrete topology {EMPTY-SET, PTS} -- non-Hausdorff, hence non-metrizable. The substantive instances are non-Hausdorff spectra (e.g. the primitive-ideal / hull-kernel space of a C*-algebra). Certifies the Hausdorff hypothesis of the gauge characterization (T2) is essential.

metrizable-iff-gauge-countable

forall([s], is-top-space(s) implies is-metrizable-top-space(s) iff is-hausdorff(s) and is-gauge-countable(s))

Warrant (well-known): A topological space is metrizable iff it is Hausdorff and its topology is generated by a countable family of pseudometrics. (=>) a metric is a single pseudometric, so {DIST} is a countable (singleton) family generating the same topology, and a metric space is Hausdorff. (<=) given such a family and X Hausdorff -- so the family separates points (T0 already suffices) -- the sum d = SUM_n 2^-n d_n/(1+d_n) is a metric inducing the same topology. Standard metrization of a countable gauge / uniform space (Kelley, General Topology, ch. 6; Bourbaki, Topologie Generale IX).

submodule-of-free-is-free

forall([md], is-module(md) and is-euclidean-ring(scal(md)) implies forall([n in nn, u in mat(n, 1, vec(md))], generates(md, n, u) and rel-free(md, n, u) implies forall([sm], is-submodule(md, sm) implies forsome([k], (k in nn and k <= n) and forsome([w in mat(k, 1, vec(md))], spans(md, k, w, sm) and rel-free(md, k, w))))))

Warrant (reference): linear-algebra.tex cor:22195-45888-207248. submodule-fg gives generators v_1..v_m of sm with m <= n; generates-coeff-matrix writes v = A.u for an m-by-n coefficient matrix A. smith-staircase supplies invertible V, U with B = V A U^-1 diagonal and its NONZERO diagonal entries b_1..b_k an initial segment (k <= min(m,n) <= n). free-transport: e = U.u still freely generates md. generates-transport: V.v still generates sm. But V.v = B.e, whose i-th entry is b_i.e_i -- so b_1.e_1 .. b_k.e_k generate sm (rows past k are 0). They are REL-FREE: if sum c_i.(b_i.e_i) = 0 then sum (c_i b_i).e_i = 0, so each c_i b_i = 0 by REL-FREE of e, and b_i /= 0 forces c_i = 0 (integral-domain-cancel-zero; a euclidean ring is an integral domain). Hence sm is free of rank k <= n. ASSEMBLY IS THE PROOF TARGET: every cited piece is installed, and the only asserted input is submodule-fg. No divisibility chain b_i | b_{i+1} is used -- that is needed only for uniqueness of the invariant factors (tex prop:22194-20879-92458).
