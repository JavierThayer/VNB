### cauchy-schwarz-finite

    forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr)], finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * b(i)), s) * finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * b(i)), s) <= finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, a(i) * a(i)), s) * finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, b(i) * b(i)), s)))

*In words:* For any finite index set S and real vectors a, b on S: the square of the dot product SUM a(i)*b(i) is at most the product of the two squared norms, (SUM a(i)^2)*(SUM b(i)^2).  Cauchy-Schwarz for finite sums, square form.

*Warrant (well-known):* Cauchy-Schwarz for finite sums:  (SUM a_i b_i)^2 <= (SUM a_i^2)(SUM b_i^2).
   The difference is the Lagrange identity SUM_{i<j} (a_i b_j - a_j b_i)^2 >= 0
   (a finite sum of squares).  Square form -- the root form is
   cauchy-schwarz-sqrt below (now that SQRT exists, real-powers.scm).

### holder-finite

    forall([s], s in set and card(s) in nn implies forall([a in fun(s, rr), b in fun(s, rr), p], p in qq and 1 < p implies forall([q], q in qq and 1 < q implies (/)(1, p) + (/)(1, q) = 1 implies finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, abs(a(i) * b(i))), s) <= rpow(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, rpow(abs(a(i)), p)), s), (/)(1, p)) * rpow(finsum(commutative-ring-additive-ag(rr-normed-field), vnb-lambda(i, s, rpow(abs(b(i)), q)), s), (/)(1, q)))))

*In words:* For any finite index set S, real vectors a, b on S, and conjugate exponents p,q>1 (rational, 1/p+1/q=1): the sum of |a(i)*b(i)| over S is at most the product of the p-norm of a and the q-norm of b -- (SUM |a_i|^p)^(1/p) times (SUM |b_i|^q)^(1/q).  Holder's inequality for finite sums.

*Warrant (well-known):* Hoelder's inequality:  SUM |a_i b_i| <= (SUM |a_i|^p)^(1/p) (SUM |b_i|^q)^(1/q)

   on the normalised vectors, summed.  Cauchy-Schwarz is the p=q=2 case.  Zero
   entries are handled by the 0^p=0 convention (rpow-zero-base).

### choose-times-factorial

    forall([n in nn, m], m in nn and m <= n implies choose(n, m) * factorial(m) = falling(n, m))

*Warrant (well-known):* Both sides count the injections of an m-element set into an n-element set.
   (i) Each injection ORD-SEGMENT(m) -> ORD-SEGMENT(n) factors uniquely as a
   choice of m-element image A subset ORD-SEGMENT(n) (CHOOSE(n,m) of them, with
   card-image-injection making |A| = m) followed by a bijection ORD-SEGMENT(m)
   onto A (m! = CARD(PERMUTATIONS m) of them); so the count is CHOOSE(n,m) * m!.
   (ii) injection-count-falling gives the same count as FALLING(n,m) =
   n(n-1)...(n-m+1).  Equate the two counts.

### injection-count-falling

    forall([n in nn, m], m in nn and m <= n implies card(injection(ord-segment(m), ord-segment(n))) = falling(n, m))

*Warrant (well-known):* Induction on m via injection-extension-recurrence.  Base: m = 0,
   |INJECTION(EMPTY-SET, C)| = 1 = FALLING(n,0) (injection-from-empty,
   ORD-SEGMENT 0 = EMPTY-SET).  Step: a domain of size m into a codomain of
   size n leaves n - m free targets, so extending the domain by one point
   multiplies the count by (n - m): exactly FALLING(n,succ m) = (n-m)*FALLING(n,m).

### injection-extension-recurrence

    forall([a, c, b, m], not(b in a) and a in set and c in set and card(a) in nn and m in nn and card(c) = card(a) + m implies card(injection(union(a, pair(b, b)), c)) = m * card(injection(a, c)))

*Warrant (informal):* Counting injections by extending the domain one point.  An injection from
   a union {b} into c restricts to an injection from a into c, and then sends b
   to any c-point not already in the image; card(a) points are used, leaving
   card(c) - card(a) = m choices, independent of the restriction.  Hence
   m * card(injection(a, c)).  The subtraction-free hypothesis card(c) =
   card(a) + m supplies m directly.

### permutation-recurrence

    forall([n in nn], card(permutations(succ(n))) = succ(n) * card(permutations(n)))

*Warrant (informal):* The factorial recurrence.  A permutation of a succ(n)-element set is fixed
   by the image of the new point (succ(n) choices) together with a permutation
   of the remaining n (card(permutations(n)) each), and the two choices are
   independent: card(permutations(succ n)) = succ(n) * card(permutations(n)).
   Base case permutations-zero.

### absolute-summable-implies-summable

    forall([grp], is-normed-ag(grp) and is-complete(nag-metric-space(grp)) implies forall([f], f in fun(dom(f), carr(grp)) and is-absolutely-summable(grp, f) implies is-summable(grp, f)))

*Warrant (informal):* Absolute summability makes the norm-tails vanish: ESUM(i |-> ||f(i)||) is a
   real, so by esum-finite-iff-bounded its finite partial sums are bounded and
   approach their sup -- for every eps there is a finite fin <= DOM f with
   Sum_{DOM f minus fin} ||f|| < eps.  Then for any finite ext with fin <= ext
   <= DOM f, ||Sum_ext f - Sum_fin f|| <= Sum_{ext minus fin} ||f|| < eps by
   the triangle inequality (subadditivity of the group norm).  Hence the net
   of finite partial sums is Cauchy in NAG-METRIC-SPACE(grp); completeness
   (IS-COMPLETE) supplies a limit r in the carrier, and that r witnesses
   SUMS-TO(grp, f, r), so IS-SUMMABLE(grp, f).  The sum is unconditional by
   construction (SUMS-TO is a net over finite subsets, not a series).  A
   candidate to discharge into a formal `proof' later.

### dominated-convergence

    forall([omega, ca, mu, cf, f, g], is-measure(omega, ca, mu) implies cf in fun(nn, fun(omega, rr-pos-star)) implies forall([n_ in nn], is-measurable-fn(omega, ca, cf(n_))) implies is-measurable-fn(omega, ca, f) implies is-measurable-fn(omega, ca, g) implies forall([n_ in nn], ptwise-le(omega, cf(n_), g)) implies integral(omega, ca, mu, g) in rr implies forall([x_ in omega], econverges-to(vnb-lambda(n_, nn, (cf(n_))(x_)), f(x_))) implies econverges-to(vnb-lambda(n_, nn, integral(omega, ca, mu, cf(n_))), integral(omega, ca, mu, f)))

*In words:* Lebesgue's dominated convergence theorem: if measurable f_k converge
   pointwise to f and are all dominated by a measurable g of FINITE integral,
   then the integrals of the f_k converge to the integral of f.  Thayer
   Prop. 2.28 (p. 18); Rudin Thm. 1.34 (p. 26).  VNB's statement is the
   [0,+inf]-valued one and differs from both sources in three recorded ways.
   (i) Both state it for real- or complex-valued integrable functions, with
   |f_k| <= g; with values in [0,+inf] the absolute value is the function
   itself, so domination is PTWISE-LE.  (ii) Both conclude the stronger
   'integral of |f - f_k| tends to 0', which needs a signed integral VNB does
   not have; VNB concludes only the convergence of the integrals.  (iii) Both
   allow the hypotheses to hold almost everywhere, VNB requires them
   everywhere, there being no almost-everywhere vocabulary yet.  Finiteness of
   the dominating integral is written 'INTEGRAL(...) in RR', RR being exactly
   the finite part of [0,+inf].

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 2.28

### dual-is-normed-vector-space

    forall([m], is-normed-vector-space(m) implies is-normed-vector-space(dual(m)))

*In words:* For every normed vector space m, the tuple DUAL(m) -- the bounded linear
   functionals on m, with pointwise addition, negation and real scaling, and the
   operator norm DUAL-NORM -- is itself a normed vector space.

*Warrant (reference):* The dual of a normed vector space is a normed vector space.  The bounded
   linear functionals are closed under pointwise sum, negation and real scaling
   (each operation preserves additivity, homogeneity and boundedness, the bound
   constants adding resp. scaling), so DUAL-VEC(m) with those operations is a
   real vector space with the zero functional as its zero.  The operator norm is
   a norm on it: DUAL-NORM(m,f) >= 0 by definition; DUAL-NORM(m,f) = 0 forces
   |f(x)| <= 0 for every x, hence f = 0; DUAL-NORM(m, r.f) = |r| DUAL-NORM(m,f)
   since c bounds |f| iff |r|c bounds |r f|; and DUAL-NORM(m, f+g) <=
   DUAL-NORM(m,f) + DUAL-NORM(m,g) because the sum of the two bounds is a bound
   for the sum and DUAL-NORM is the LEAST bound.  Standard: Rudin, Functional
   Analysis, 4.1; Conway, A Course in Functional Analysis, III.1.

### fatou

*In words:* Fatou's lemma: the integral of the pointwise lower limit is at most the

### hahn-banach-seminorm

    forall([m, p, s, f, fld], is-module(m) implies is-normed-field(fld) implies scal(m) = normed-field-as-commutative-ring(fld) implies is-seminorm(m, p) implies is-submodule(m, s) implies is-k-linear-on(m, s, f) implies forall([x in s], (fnrm(fld))(f(x)) <= p(x)) implies forsome([ff], is-k-linear(m, ff) and extends-on(s, ff, f) and forall([x in vec(m)], (fnrm(fld))(ff(x)) <= p(x))))

### monotone-convergence

    forall([omega, ca, mu, cf], is-measure(omega, ca, mu) implies cf in fun(nn, fun(omega, rr-pos-star)) implies forall([n_ in nn], is-measurable-fn(omega, ca, cf(n_))) implies forall([n_ in nn], ptwise-le(omega, cf(n_), cf(succ(n_)))) implies is-measurable-fn(omega, ca, ptwise-sup(omega, cf)) and integral(omega, ca, mu, ptwise-sup(omega, cf)) = esup(image(vnb-lambda(n_, nn, integral(omega, ca, mu, cf(n_))), nn)))

*In words:* Lebesgue's monotone convergence theorem: for a non-decreasing sequence of
   measurable [0,+inf]-valued functions, the pointwise supremum is measurable
   and its integral is the supremum of the integrals.  Thayer Prop. 2.16
   (p. 14), and property (7) of Thm. 2.7 (p. 10), which states it with sup on
   both sides exactly as here; Rudin Thm. 1.26 (p. 21) states it with limits.
   VNB follows Thayer's sup form -- the sequences are monotone, so the two
   agree, and [0,+inf] has ESUP but no limit.

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 2.16

### open-mapping-bounded-inverse

    forall([m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt, gg], is-frechet-structure(m1, fam1) implies is-frechet-structure(m2, fam2) implies is-cont-lin(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt) implies is-linear-map(m2, m1, gg) implies forall([x in vec(m1)], gg(tt(x)) = x) implies forall([y in vec(m2)], tt(gg(y)) = y) implies is-cont-lin(m2, fam2, m1, fam1, gg))

*In words:* Open mapping theorem, bounded-inverse form: a continuous linear bijection tt
   between Frechet spaces (with linear two-sided inverse gg) has continuous inverse
   gg.  A corollary of `open-mapping-theorem': openness of tt is continuity of gg.
   Sources: Yosida II.5; the user's TVS notes, Thm 4.7.  Uses Baire.

*Warrant (reference):* Yosida, Functional Analysis, Corollary of the open mapping theorem, Ch. II.6

### open-mapping-theorem

    forall([m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt], is-frechet-structure(m1, fam1) implies is-frechet-structure(m2, fam2) implies is-cont-lin(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt) implies forall([y in vec(m2)], forsome([x in vec(m1)], y = tt(x))) implies is-open-lin-map(m1, fam1, m2, fam2, tt))

*In words:* Open mapping theorem (Banach): a continuous linear surjection tt between Frechet
   spaces is open.  Stated where openness of a LINEAR map actually lives -- at 0:
   the image of the basic neighbourhood FR-BALL(m1, fam1, n, eps) contains a basic
   neighbourhood FR-BALL(m2, fam2, n', eps').  No open-set quantification is needed
   and none occurs in the proof.  In Schaefer's vocabulary (III.1, p. 74) this says
   tt is a TOPOLOGICAL HOMOMORPHISM onto m2.  Sources: the user's TVS notes Thm 4.7;
   Schaefer III.2 (Banach's homomorphism theorem, p. 76); Yosida II.5 (p. 75, for
   F-spaces, i.e. without local convexity).  Those three prove more than is stated
   here: the codomain need only be a Hausdorff TVS and tt's RANGE need only be
   non-meager, whence tt is surjective and the codomain complete metrizable.  Here
   surjectivity is assumed and the codomain is assumed Frechet -- meagerness
   relative to a seminorm family is not in the base.  Uses Baire.

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Topological Vector Spaces (notes), Theorem 4.7

## Set & quotient constructions  (4)

### caratheodory-measurable-sigma-algebra

    forall([omega, mu], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies is-sigma-algebra(omega, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu)))

*In words:* Caratheodory's theorem, first half: the sets that split every test set
   additively for an outer measure mu form a sigma-algebra on omega.  Thayer
   Prop. 4.4 (p. 30) states both halves in one sentence ('the mu* measurable
   sets form a (non-empty) sigma-algebra A and mu*|A is a countably additive
   measure'); VNB splits it into this and caratheodory-measure, because the
   two conclusions are about different objects and a proof will want them
   separately.  Rudin does not carry the Caratheodory construction in
   Chapter 1, so no Rudin number is given.

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 4.4

### caratheodory-measure

    forall([omega, mu], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies is-measure(omega, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu), res(mu, caratheodory-sets(omega, mu))))

*In words:* Caratheodory's theorem, second half: the restriction of the outer measure
   mu to the sigma-algebra of its measurable sets is a countably additive
   measure.  Thayer Prop. 4.4 (p. 30), written there as mu*|A; VNB writes the
   restriction with the kernel's RES head.  See
   caratheodory-measurable-sigma-algebra for why the source's one statement is
   two here.

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Prop. 4.4

### caratheodory-null-measurable

    forall([omega, mu, es], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies es subset omega implies mu(es) = 0 implies is-caratheodory-measurable(omega, mu, es))

*In words:* Every set of outer measure zero is Caratheodory measurable.  Thayer
   Remark 4.3 (p. 30), stated there as 'every set E such that mu*(E) = 0 is
   measurable'; VNB adds the explicit hypothesis that es is a subset of omega,
   which the source carries implicitly by working inside P(X).

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Remark 4.3

### caratheodory-superadditive-suffices

    forall([omega, mu, es], is-outer-measure(omega, mu) implies es subset omega implies forall([a_ in power(omega)], eplus(mu(intersection(a_, es)), mu(difference(a_, es))) <= mu(a_)) implies is-caratheodory-measurable(omega, mu, es))

*In words:* Only one of the two inequalities has to be checked: if the two pieces of
   every test set together weigh AT MOST the test set, then es is Caratheodory
   measurable, the reverse inequality being automatic from subadditivity.
   Thayer states this as the displayed inequality (75) in the paragraph after
   Def. 4.2 (p. 30) -- an unnumbered remark, so the citation is to the
   equation, not to a numbered result.  This is the working form of the
   measurability test and the reason it is stated at all.

*Warrant (reference):* Thayer, Construction of Measures (notes), Eq. (75), following Def. 4.2

### gauge-countable-not-metrizable-witness

    forsome([s], is-gauge-countable(s) and not(is-metrizable-top-space(s)))

*Warrant (well-known):* There is a gauge-countable space that is not metrizable, so IS-GAUGE-COUNTABLE
   without IS-HAUSDORFF does not imply IS-METRIZABLE-TOP-SPACE.  Concrete witness:
   the single pseudometric d == 0 on any carrier with at least two points is a
   countable (singleton) pseudometric family whose gauge topology is the indiscrete

   substantive instances are non-Hausdorff spectra (e.g. the primitive-ideal /
   hull-kernel space of a C*-algebra).  Certifies the Hausdorff hypothesis of the
   gauge characterization (T2) is essential.

### metrizable-iff-gauge-countable

    forall([s], is-top-space(s) implies is-metrizable-top-space(s) iff is-hausdorff(s) and is-gauge-countable(s))

*Warrant (well-known):* A topological space is metrizable iff it is Hausdorff and its topology is
   generated by a countable family of pseudometrics.  (=>) a metric is a single
   pseudometric, so {DIST} is a countable (singleton) family generating the same
   topology, and a metric space is Hausdorff.  (<=) given such a family and X
   Hausdorff -- so the family separates points (T0 already suffices) -- the sum
   d = SUM_n 2^-n d_n/(1+d_n) is a metric inducing the same topology.  Standard
   metrization of a countable gauge / uniform space (Kelley, General Topology,
   ch. 6; Bourbaki, Topologie Generale IX).

### submodule-of-free-is-free

    forall([md], is-module(md) and is-euclidean-ring(scal(md)) implies forall([n in nn, u in mat(n, 1, vec(md))], generates(md, n, u) and rel-free(md, n, u) implies forall([sm], is-submodule(md, sm) implies forsome([k], (k in nn and k <= n) and forsome([w in mat(k, 1, vec(md))], spans(md, k, w, sm) and rel-free(md, k, w))))))

*Warrant (reference):* linear-algebra.tex cor:22195-45888-207248.  submodule-fg gives generators
   v_1..v_m of sm with m <= n; generates-coeff-matrix writes v = A.u for an
   m-by-n coefficient matrix A.  smith-staircase supplies invertible V, U with
   B = V A U^-1 diagonal and its NONZERO diagonal entries b_1..b_k an initial
   segment (k <= min(m,n) <= n).  free-transport: e = U.u still freely generates
   md.  generates-transport: V.v still generates sm.  But V.v = B.e, whose i-th
   entry is b_i.e_i -- so b_1.e_1 .. b_k.e_k generate sm (rows past k are 0).
   They are REL-FREE: if sum c_i.(b_i.e_i) = 0 then sum (c_i b_i).e_i = 0, so
   each c_i b_i = 0 by REL-FREE of e, and b_i /= 0 forces c_i = 0
   (integral-domain-cancel-zero; a euclidean ring is an integral domain).
   Hence sm is free of rank k <= n.  ASSEMBLY IS THE PROOF TARGET: every cited
   piece is installed, and the only asserted input is submodule-fg.
   No divisibility chain b_i | b_{i+1} is used -- that is needed only for
   uniqueness of the invariant factors (tex prop:22194-20879-92458).
